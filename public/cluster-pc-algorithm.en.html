How does Clustered PC Anomaly Detection make its verdict?

This page uses figures to explain how the wafers shown on the Defect SPIDER "Clustered PC Anomaly Detection" screen are selected.

In one line · If defects in one spot of a wafer are 3 or more times denser than elsewhere, it is judged as Clustered PC. Even with many defects, a wafer is not clustered if they are spread evenly across it.

The wafer figures in this document use synthetic data made for explanation. However, the verdicts, circles and ratios in each figure come from running the actual production judgment program as is.

The verdict takes 4 steps

In the daily judgment that runs once a day, the same 4 steps below are applied to every measured wafer.

1Clean up data

Defect coordinates (positions relative to the wafer center) are cleaned up for judgment.

0.05mm is about half the thickness of a human hair. Two points within this distance are treated as the same defect recorded twice and counted as one.

2Find clusters

Circles (magnifying glasses) of several sizes are placed all over the wafer to find spots where a circle holds unusually many defects.

5 circle sizes are used

Small circles catch tight, point-like clusters; large circles catch widely spread clustering. The dashed circle is the center area (radius about 53mm, 1/8 of the wafer area).

The 10mm circle is used only outside the center area and the 25mm circle only inside it. This prevents 10mm circles from splitting up defects gathered in the center, which would make the 25mm circle miss the clustering.

What "unusually many" means: the baseline

Defects are often naturally more common near the wafer edge. So each spot is compared with other cells at the same distance (the same ring) to set the baseline for that spot.

The 2 highest cells are excluded because a cluster that raises the baseline would end up being missed. Also, so that a wafer with few defects does not get a baseline near 0 (where just a few gathered points would count as a cluster), the baseline never goes below half of the wafer-wide average.

3 conditions to count as a cluster

4 or moreDefects inside the circle2–3 adjacent points are common, so they are not counted.
3x or moreCompared with the baselineIt must be at least 3 times the baseline for that spot.
Not by chanceHard to explain by luckThe chance of this many defects gathering even if they fell randomly must be very low.

Because circles are placed thousands of times, the third condition is set strictly to account for "occasional hits". However, large circles (50·75mm) at the edge (120mm or more from the center) are judged a little more leniently so that edge clustering on wafers with few defects is also caught.

Multiple clusters are found one by one

3Compare with other areas

Finding a cluster does not immediately mean clustering. The defect count of the area containing the cluster is compared with the average of other areas of the same size to calculate how many times higher it is (the clustering ratio). Every compared area is the same size: 1/8 of the wafer area.

Cluster outside the center area: sector comparison

Cluster inside the center area: ring comparison

The center area is hard to divide by direction, so the center circle is compared with the outer rings.

Why is comparison needed?

Even with the same cluster, if there are many defects elsewhere too, it is hard to say "only that spot is the problem". The upper-left clusters on the two wafers below are identical.

4Final verdict

If any of A, B or C below applies, the wafer is Clustered PC. The threshold ratio is 3x for all of them.

RuleWhich clustersRequired condition
AWide clusters found with 50mm·75mm radius circles (any position)1 or more, clustering ratio 3x or more
BClusters found with the 25mm circle inside the center area1 or more, ring comparison 3x or more
CSmall clusters found with 1.5mm·10mm circles10 or more (ratio and position are not checked)
Otherwise9 or fewer small clusters, or a ratio below 3xNormal
Production data showed that wafers with only one or two small clusters were mostly normal. So small clusters count as clustering only when they repeat in many places (10 or more).

Good to know

Are wafers with few defects rarely caught?
Yes. Wafers with fewer than 10 are not judged, and those with fewer than about 20 are hard to distinguish from chance, so they are rarely caught. This is a range that is hard to judge even by eye.
Are very tiny repeated points (within 0.05mm) caught?
No. Points overlapping within 0.05mm are counted as one, so they are not caught. This choice reduces false verdicts caused by duplicate records.
Is the 3x threshold final?
It is being tuned based on production results. If a verdict is wrong, please click Not clustered PC on the card (or Clustered PC if it is right). The collected verdicts are used to tune the criteria.
What about wafers that went through multiple tools?
The verdict is based only on wafer coordinates. On screen, the wafer is grouped by each tool (EQPID) it went through.
